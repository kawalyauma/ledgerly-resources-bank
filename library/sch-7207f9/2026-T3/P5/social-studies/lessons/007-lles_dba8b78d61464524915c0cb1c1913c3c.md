# TOPIC: THE GOVERNMENT OF UGANDA
## SUB-TOPIC: Officers of Parliament and terms used in Parliament

### The Speaker and Deputy Speaker
The **Speaker of Parliament** is the presiding officer who chairs parliamentary debates and meetings. The Speaker maintains the formal proceedings of Parliament and leads its sittings. The **Deputy Speaker** chairs meetings and debates when the Speaker is absent. These officers enable the assembly to carry out its legislative business. The Speaker is also the official head of the Legislature. Parliament's work includes discussion, making laws and consideration of the national budget, so its meetings need an officer to lead them.

### The Clerk and Sergeant at Arms
The **Clerk to Parliament** is an administrative officer who records and keeps proceedings and records of Parliament. The written record of parliamentary proceedings is known as the **Hansard**. The **Sergeant at Arms** is responsible for security and order in Parliament. The Sergeant at Arms alerts members when the Speaker is about to enter and carries the mace. The Clerk and Sergeant at Arms support the formal work of Parliament, but they have different responsibilities: one keeps records while the other deals with order and security.

### Important parliamentary terms
A **bill** is a proposed law presented in Parliament. An **Act** is a law passed by Parliament and signed by the President. A bill therefore becomes an Act after the required approval and signature. The **Hansard** is the written or recorded proceedings of Parliament. The **mace** is a symbol of authority representing the power of the Speaker and Parliament. These terms are important for understanding what happens when Parliament debates laws and keeps records of its decisions.

### Qualifications and representation
The supplied parliamentary notes list qualifications for a **Member of Parliament**. A person should be a Ugandan citizen, a registered voter, at least eighteen years old and have completed Senior Six or an equivalent level of formal education. Members may be elected from constituencies or to represent special interest groups. Parliament has members representing districts, constituencies, women, workers, young people and people with disabilities. Members are involved in making national laws and approving public financial plans and some government appointments. They are different from the officers who organise and record Parliament's meetings.
