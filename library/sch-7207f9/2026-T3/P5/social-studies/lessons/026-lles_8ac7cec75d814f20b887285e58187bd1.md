# TOPIC: POPULATION SIZE AND DISTRIBUTION
## SUB-TOPIC: Importance of a population census, information collected and problems faced

### Meaning of census
A **population census** is the official counting of the people living in a country. It also collects information about the people being counted. The trained people who visit households for this work are called **enumerators**. The body responsible for carrying out census in Uganda is the Uganda Bureau of Statistics (UBOS). A **census night** is the night before the census exercise begins. Census data provides an organised way of understanding the population rather than depending on guesses. The government uses the information to plan for the people.

### Importance of a census
The census helps government determine the number of people living in different areas. It can be used to learn the population's age and sex structure, birth and death rates, education levels and standard of living. These details assist in planning the provision of services. Schools, health facilities and transport are among the needs that government must consider. Census information can therefore help people receive services according to their needs and the number of residents in a place.

### Information enumerators collect
During census, enumerators gather information about the members of households. The source notes list people's **age**, **sex**, **occupation**, **level of education**, religion, number of children and the type of housing they use. Information may also include sources of energy used in the home. These details describe the population and help reveal differences among areas. Enumerators gather the information from people in their households, and the resulting totals provide an important basis for population planning.

### Problems faced during a census
A census may be affected by shortage of funds, bad weather, poor transport, insecurity and long distances between homes. Some people may not understand the exercise and others may give inaccurate answers. The source describes a ten-year census interval, partly because counting is expensive and population changes need time to occur. Good transport, public understanding and the work of trained enumerators all help the exercise succeed. Census results can become less useful if information is missing or inaccurate.
